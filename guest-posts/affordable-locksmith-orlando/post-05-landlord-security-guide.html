Florida Landlord's Complete Guide to Lock Security and Tenant Turnover (2026)

If you own rental property in Florida — whether a single-family home, a duplex, or a multi-unit apartment building — your lock strategy directly impacts your legal liability, your tenants' safety, and your ability to control access to your investment. Most landlords handle rekeying as an afterthought. The ones who have been through a dispute with a former tenant know better.

This guide covers everything Florida landlords need to know: the legal requirements, the locksmith services that actually matter at turnover, master key systems for multi-unit properties, smart lock options, and what a proper per-unit security budget looks like.

Florida Law: What Landlords Are Actually Required to Provide

Quick Answer: Florida Statute §83.51 and §83.53 require functioning deadbolts, window latches, and sliding door blockers on all rental units. Florida courts and legal advisors consistently interpret the habitability standard to require rekeying between tenancies — even where the statute doesn't use that exact word.

Florida Statute §83.53 is the specific section that defines required locking devices on residential rental units:

  • A deadbolt lock on each main entry door with a bolt of at least one inch throw
  • A window latch on each accessible window
  • A secondary blocking device (bar, pin, or similar) on each sliding glass door
  • A peephole or door viewer on entry doors (if the door has no window or adjacent sidelight)

These are the statutory minimums. Note what is not listed: a requirement to rekey. However, the habitability obligation under §83.51 is where the rekeying standard effectively comes from. A unit where a prior tenant holds a functional key is not, in any meaningful sense, "habitable" or "secure." If a prior tenant uses a retained key to enter and causes harm, the landlord's exposure is severe.

What Happens If You Don't Rekey?

Florida courts have held landlords liable in cases where prior tenants retained functional keys and later re-entered the property. Beyond litigation risk, practical problems include:

  • Former tenant re-entry (property damage, theft, harassment of new tenant)
  • Insurance claim complications when entry was "unauthorized but key-enabled"
  • New tenant complaints, lease disputes, and early termination claims
  • Bad reviews and vacancy costs if the incident becomes visible

The cost to rekey a single rental unit in Orlando runs $75–$125. That is the cheapest legal and liability protection available in property management.

The Turnover Lock Checklist: Every Unit, Every Vacancy

Mandatory at Every Tenant Turnover

  • ☐ Rekey all exterior door lock cylinders (deadbolt + knob/handle)
  • ☐ Rekey or reprogram garage door keypad PIN (if applicable)
  • ☐ Recover all keys from departing tenant — document count in move-out form
  • ☐ Inspect deadbolt throw and strike plate — replace if worn or damaged
  • ☐ Test all window latches — repair or replace broken hardware
  • ☐ Test secondary bar on all sliding glass doors — reinstall or replace if missing
  • ☐ Change or reprogram any smart lock access codes (tenant codes, service codes)
  • ☐ Document new key issuance to new tenant in writing

Recommended (Not Mandatory) at Turnover

  • ☐ Inspect lock hardware grade — upgrade to Grade 1 if current locks are Grade 3
  • ☐ Apply silicone lubricant to all lock cylinders (Florida humidity accelerates corrosion)
  • ☐ Test exterior lighting and motion sensors
  • ☐ Verify that common area locks (mailboxes, laundry, pool gates) function correctly

Master Key Systems: The Right Solution for Multi-Unit Properties

What a Master Key System Does

A master key system is a purpose-designed lock hierarchy where one key (the master) opens every lock in the building, while each tenant's key opens only their own unit. In a properly designed system, there is no single key that "opens everything" except the one you hold — tenants cannot copy their key to access other units, and there is no risk of key loss cascading into a building-wide security failure.

For any property with three or more units, common areas, or shared amenity spaces (laundry room, storage, gym, pool gate), a master key system dramatically simplifies property management.

Restricted Keyways: The Security Upgrade Worth Considering

A standard master key system can be compromised by tenants copying keys at a hardware store. A restricted keyway system uses patented key blanks that cannot be duplicated without authorization from the locksmith on record. For higher-turnover properties or areas with known security concerns, restricted keyways eliminate unauthorized key duplication entirely.

The cost premium for restricted keyway hardware is modest — typically $15–$25 per lock cylinder more than standard — and pays for itself the first time you avoid a security incident caused by an unauthorized key copy.

What Landlords Get Wrong About Lock Security

Mistake 1: Only Rekeying the Deadbolt

Many landlords rekey the deadbolt and leave the knob lock unchanged. A knob lock that still accepts the prior tenant's key is a security gap. Both lock cylinders on every exterior door should be rekeyed at every turnover.

Mistake 2: Trusting Tenants to Return All Keys

No key return policy prevents tenants from copying keys before move-out. The only way to ensure all prior keys are disabled is to rekey — not to collect them.

Mistake 3: Using Cheap Padlocks on Storage or Utility Areas

Grade 3 padlocks and low-quality deadbolts on storage areas, meter rooms, and utility closets are frequently targeted in rental property break-ins. These areas often contain HVAC equipment, electrical panels, and tenant belongings. Grade 1 padlocks with hardened shackles cost $25–$45 and are significantly more resistant to attack.

Mistake 4: Ignoring Sliding Glass Door Security

Standard sliding glass door factory locks fail at moderate force. Florida statute requires a secondary blocking device, but many landlords install a cut wooden dowel that falls out or gets removed. A purpose-built sliding door bar lock ($15–$30) with a foot-operated bracket stays in position reliably and is far harder to defeat than a loose wooden rod.

Mistake 5: Not Documenting Lock Hardware

Keep a written record of the lock brand, model, grade, and cylinder type for every unit. When you need a replacement cylinder or a rekey at 11 PM after a lockout call, having this information ready saves time and money. A simple spreadsheet per property is sufficient.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a Florida landlord legally required to rekey between tenants?

Florida Statute §83.51 requires landlords to maintain premises in a habitable condition, which legal advisors consistently interpret to include ensuring prior tenants cannot re-enter. While §83.51 doesn't use the word "rekey," courts have held landlords liable for incidents caused by prior tenant key access. Rekeying between every tenancy is the standard recommended by Florida property attorneys and is the baseline for professional property management in the state.

How much does it cost to rekey a rental property in Orlando, FL?

A standard Orlando rental unit with 2–3 exterior locks costs $75–$125 to rekey. Multi-unit properties with a property manager account and volume scheduling typically reduce the per-unit cost to $50–$90. Smart lock code changes cost $0 per turnover after the initial installation ($150–$350 per door depending on hardware model and labor).

What is a master key system and should I use one for my rental property?

A master key system allows one master key to open all locks in a building while individual tenant keys only open their own unit. For multi-family properties, it simplifies maintenance access and eliminates the need to carry multiple keys. Setup costs $150–$750 depending on property size, and rekeying at turnover is faster and cheaper within an existing master key system.

What locks are legally required on rental units in Florida?

Florida Statute §83.53 requires a deadbolt with at least a one-inch throw on each main entry door, a window latch on each accessible window, and a secondary blocking device on each sliding glass door. These are minimum legal requirements. Most property attorneys and insurance providers recommend Grade 1 ANSI/BHMA deadbolts on all exterior doors, which exceed the statutory minimum and reduce liability exposure.
